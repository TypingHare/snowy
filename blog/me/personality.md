## Personality

Two things about personality have baffled me for over a decade: what, in general, shapes a person’s personality, and what exactly my own personality is. Neither question has a straightforward answer, but apparently the latter is easier to explore, or at least to gather clues about, because no one understands you better than you understand yourself.

However, trying to understand myself fully means frequently asking myself questions and coming up with appropriate answers. One of the most noticeable side effects is indescribable mental exhaustion. Besides, many people find it irksome when someone overexplains themselves, especially when those explanations differ greatly from how others perceive them.

Even setting those factors aside, trying to perceive your own personality by simply proposing explanations for feelings arising from internal and unconscious processes is an inferior approach. Personalities come from comparisons between two groups of people who behave differently under the same circumstances. For example, the pair of words "introversion" and "extroversion" describes the extent of how much pleasure an individual can gain in a group setting, such as a party. If everyone happened to experience the same level of pleasure in an identical setting, these two words would never have appeared in dictionaries.

Therefore, a better way to understand oneself is to better understand others, particularly the differences in their internal feelings and motivations across different situations. Based on my limited experience, this is extremely difficult for most people, including me. Many of us naively think, “If I can do it, then others can too by following my methodology.” Humans are good at noticing physical differences in others, so they would not accuse a person with rickets of running slowly. But when it comes to differences in mental and internal abilities, we can hardly put ourselves in others’ shoes, often believing that their inability stems from laziness or a lack of ambition.

Furthermore, you will soon realize that understanding other people’s personalities also means figuring out what shapes them. Modern biology and psychology suggest that both genetics and the environment in which a person grows up shape their personality, just as they influence other biological traits. Because genetic factors are largely inaccessible and difficult to analyze directly, it is often more practical to focus on environmental factors, including family wealth, the neighborhood in which a person was raised, and the individual experiences they have had.

Okay, now let's formalize the problem. What we want is to identify the underlying relationship between the environment in which a person grows up ($E$) and their outward behaviors ($B$), given that genetics ($G$) are inaccessible latent variables. Define two functions, $f_E$ and $f_G$:

$$
B = f_E(E) + f_G(G) + \epsilon.
$$

Here, $\epsilon$ represents residual variation. Our goal is to identify the component of behavior that is primarily influenced by the environment, so that we can predict a person's behaviors based on their environment:

$$
\hat{B}_E = f_E(E).
$$

This mental model is important to me because it constantly reminds me to understand a person’s past and the environment in which they grew up before judging them, either implicitly or explicitly.

### Introversion (69%)

My solitary style is quite noticeable that many people surrounding me first thought that I'm extremely introverted. But the percentage tells that I'm not that much. In fact, I recently discovered that I'm relatively extraverted among introverted people, because I can always talk nonstop in an one-on-one conversation.

But, I'm only interested in topics requiring deep thinking and allowing my mind to spread out quite a bit. I don't like gathering shallow information, and hence I can't feel showing of uninterested when people keep throwing [八卦消息] at me one after one.

When I was in high school, I was often considered as an extrovert, as I often offered help to peers and talk about a problem for a long time, no matter with whom. But all of that came from an illusion because of my altruism, a big distingushable part in my personality. When I stay in a community where most are strongly connected, I find it fun to help others out, especially if there is a chance to show off some of my talents.

Hence, I wasn't that introverted until I reached the junior year, when most Chinese people were locked down home for several months due to the COVID-19 pandemic. During that period, I taught myself a lot of knowledge from all fields. I kept hearing news that the nation-wise lockdown make many people suffer from depression because they can hardly connect with others. As opposed of those people, I couldn't feel any sense of loneliness, and instead, enjoyed immersing in my own world of exploring unknown.

But that doesn't mean I don't need socializing. Later in 2021, after I immigrated to the United States with my parents, I can hardly contact my friends on WeChat thanks to time difference. Besides, a miserable thing that I don't want to talk about made me become unprecedentedly eagar to talk to others. However, I was out of school and my green card hadn't arrived, so I couldn't get a job legally at that time. It is really hard to explain how I got over with the half year, until our family moved to the great Boston.

Because of the trauma I got at that time, I shut down my "emotional module" and became quite emotionless. In the following five years, I often left a terrible first impression to others with my cold face expressions. Only introverted thinkers, such as Jack (also INTJ) and Andrew, saw my witness through my eyes, sensed my wisdom from my "low-quality" English expressions. They eventually became my good friends.

Something in my personality doesn't change over time, which makes me an "ambivalent" introvert. First, I hate staying in noisy places for a long time, as I often need to think quitely. Noise environments have great impact on my thinking ability, making me feel my internal supports gradually fall apart. Nevertheless, numbing my frontier cortex, such as drinking alcohol, can temporarily disable such feeling. Second, I don't like to go to parties, meetings, conferences full of people that I don't know. I need at least one acquaintance in those environments. Third, I feel uncomfortable in a discussion where people speak randomly instead of doing it in-turn.

### Intuition (85%)

I remember vaguely that my first test on MBTI said that my intuition was around 70%. Over time, my intuition becomes stronger, and my major, computer science, must have a great influence on it.

There are two science fields that I enjoy the most, one is biology, the other is computer science. I like the former because it tells me how surrendipidous our we humans are, and gives me a reason to cherish it. I love the latter because I can turn my whimsical ideas into reality at will. These two fields have a great common in complicated and intricate architecture that require abstract thinking and pattern recognition ability to better understand them.

Since I was kid, creating new things by combining possessed materials with existing tools has been a great pleasure of mine. One thing that I'm weirder than other kids was that I often give up what I already built and started from scratch again, if I was not satisfied with the current design. It turns out that in many situations, I care more about the perfectness in design over reaching the goal. Or, the intention of starting to create things is to seek a wonderful design instead of really getting to somewhere. In that sense, I enjoy the intermediate process more than many people. 

However, such perfectionism often bothers me and others, because I value architecture very much and spend much time designing it at the beginning without having any deliverables. Others think I'm lazy and keep finding execuses for not moving forward, while I defense myself with the significance of having a great foundation where things are built on top of.

But why do I have such strong willing to build a good architecture? I think the most reliable reason is that I can easily sense the flaws in an architecture and forsee a scenario where it will fail with my strong intuition. When I was younger, I believe others have the same capability and I often wondered why they can tolerate those defects.

Later, I found the answer. First, most people want the outcomes instead of a good architecture, as nobody really cares. Second, many people don't have strong intuition to perceive existing defects. Third, even though some of them are aware of those problems, they find it hard to come up with a solution, so they eventually give up. For the last part, thinking plays an important role, and that draws a noticeable line between NT and NF.
