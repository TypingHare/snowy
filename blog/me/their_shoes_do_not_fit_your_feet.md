# Their Shoes Do Not Fit Your Feet

“Sorry, I’m kinda introverted. I’m probably not going (to an event).”

“Then try to learn how to be more extroverted. You can’t stay in your comfort zone your entire life.”

I’m sick of dialogues like this, which have come up repeatedly from time to time since I was a kid. Still, I always thought they were right. But recently, I came up with a framework that can defend my position a bit.

It is noticeable that humans in general are poor at understanding things they can’t visualize. One example is understanding other people’s minds. Because we can receive only very limited information through others’ eyes and body language, we usually communicate through language, which, as I will discuss later, is far from fully reflecting one’s mind. For this reason, we have often been told to put ourselves in other people’s shoes. Confucius put this more straightforwardly: “Don’t do to others what you don’t want done to yourself.” (己所不欲，勿施于人.) More specifically, this technique asks you to keep thinking about one question: “If I were them, how would I feel about it?”

Unfortunately, this doesn’t hold all the time. In other words, there is a vague boundary beyond which it is no longer applicable.

## Preference-Constraint Table (PCT)

I don’t like eating seafood, and coincidentally, I’m allergic to some of it, such as lobster and crab. Once, my best friend brought me to a famous seafood restaurant in Boston. He is somewhat of a cold person, and a smile rarely appears on his face. But that day, an unusual smile had been on his face ever since we started waiting in line. Perhaps the fishy smell coming out of the kitchen triggered his brain to release dopamine. I was confused, without realizing that even greater confusion would come later.

He ordered two big plates of raw oysters. Because I had emphasized that I didn’t eat raw seafood, he also ordered a plate of cooked oysters. I got myself a seafood ramen, seemingly the only acceptable dish in the restaurant.

Soon, the dishes arrived, and his smile gradually widened like that of some evil character in a Disney cartoon. But the strong fishy smell coming from the raw oysters started to make me feel uncomfortable. I felt as if my stomach were undergoing some weird chemical reactions. He grabbed an oyster and slid it into his mouth without any hesitation, and shortly afterward, a look of pleasure began to emerge on his face.

“Try this. It’s extremely fresh and tasty,” he said.

“I’m good. I’ll eat some cooked ones,” I declined politely. But he insisted: “This is a rare opportunity. You won’t understand how good it is until you try the first one.”

I recalled that I had heard almost exactly the same sentence when I was asked to eat a cicada nymph in China.

Pushed by an invitation that was hard to say no to, I swallowed a raw oyster with my eyes tightly closed and my breath held. All I could taste, from my mouth to my throat to my stomach, was pure fishiness, and nothing even close to delicious.

Later, when we got home, we talked about seafood, and I said that I was allergic to some of it, though not very seriously. I can’t forget his reply: “What a pity! You lose a lot of pleasure in life, then.”

I was mad, for a reason that I couldn’t immediately put my finger on. Later, I drew the following 2×2 table:

|                 | I Like           | I Don't Like         |
| --------------- | ---------------- | -------------------- |
| **I Can Eat**   | a blessing       | just avoid it        |
| **I Can't Eat** | very unfortunate | I couldn't care less |

I had repeatedly emphasized that seafood was not my thing, but his reply still presumed that I liked it, simply because he liked it himself. The flaw in that logic was what really pissed me off. The table shown above can be generalized to any pair of preference and constraint, so I refer to it as the **preference–constraint table (PCT)**.

From then on, I often started asking others whether they liked something before making any comments, and the PCT became a really helpful tool as a more general way of understanding others than simply putting myself in their shoes.
